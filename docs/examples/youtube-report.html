Purpose and security model

Peter’s talk examined how LLMs can accelerate firmware vulnerability discovery and potentially bypass secure boot. His central argument was that proprietary firmware’s complexity and obscurity are becoming much weaker barriers to attackers.

He described secure boot as a mechanism that refuses to boot operating systems without valid digital signatures. Firmware sits between the device’s relatively trusted internal components and external inputs such as SD cards, NVMe drives, USB devices, and network boot services. Because firmware initializes hardware, handles external devices, and implements secure boot checks, vulnerabilities in code that runs before signature verification can undermine the protection.

Rather than physically extracting firmware from chips, an attacker can often obtain firmware updates online, sometimes from GitHub. Peter used Kimi for his investigation because it was willing to assist with tasks that, in his experience, other models refused.

How the analysis narrowed the search

Peter outlined a workflow that progressively reduced a large firmware image to a small number of potentially exploitable paths:

- Unpack and disassemble the firmware. Initially unreadable sections became accessible after the model identified an LZ4 decompression routine in readable code and applied it to other sections. A further proprietary data transformation was similarly identified and used to expose the remaining hidden section. Peter said the firmware was opened up within approximately five minutes. - Use embedded error messages to identify components. HTTP, DNS, and other library-specific strings provided anchors for interpreting otherwise difficult assembly and constructing a call graph. - Locate the secure boot verification boundary. The investigation focused on code processing attacker-controlled inputs before that boundary, rather than code running after an operating system had passed verification. - Map external inputs to relevant code paths. Different storage devices and network interfaces exposed different parts of the firmware. - Search for a limited set of suspicious patterns, including possible buffer overflows, problematic loops, incomplete conditional handling, logical errors, and known vulnerabilities in identifiable libraries.

This first pass produced candidate issues, not confirmed exploits. Some suspicious code could be unreachable, protected by validation, or capable only of crashing the processor.

Peter described evaluating the consequences of candidate bugs and then tracing backward from suspicious operations toward external inputs. Reverse traversal eliminated branches blocked by checks or containing unreachable code, while retaining paths connected to user-controlled data. He suggested that filtering crash-only cases could remove roughly half the initial candidates.

According to Peter, this stage was static analysis: the model inspected code and modeled control flow without executing the firmware. It then translated relevant instruction sequences into Python simulations to check whether arithmetic, data movement, or other details invalidated the apparent exploit paths. His illustrative workflow ended with approximately five legitimate vulnerabilities, though these still required a feasible way to reach them on the configured device.

From individual bugs to exploit chains

Peter emphasized the difference between finding a vulnerable routine and making real hardware invoke it. A device configured to boot only from an SD card, for example, might never enter its NVMe driver.

The next step was to combine vulnerabilities and unusual behavior into an executable chain. His example involved a network-booting compute module:

- An attacker-controlled HTTP server returned an error, sending the bootloader into a separate retry implementation. - A subsequent redirect caused execution to enter another path involving DNS resolution. - The DNS handling code copied a response into a 64-byte buffer without adequately constraining its size. - An oversized response, described as approximately 5 KB, caused memory corruption that Peter said enabled remote code execution and a complete secure boot bypass.

He attributed part of this attack surface to functionality inherited from imported libraries that had not been removed, even though the firmware did not need it for its intended operation.

Reported results and limitations

Peter said he initially expected Kimi to find nothing, but it reported 15 zero-day vulnerabilities and three secure boot escapes. He qualified the 15 findings: some might be exploitable, some merely caused crashes, and their consequences were not all established.

He reported that: - The scan took approximately one hour using a $100 Kimi subscription. - Reproducing the findings on physical hardware took him an entire day. - Human hardware validation, rather than the model’s initial analysis, was the bottleneck.

The hardware vendor and product were not disclosed. During questions, Peter jokingly referred to a nonexistent “generic blue compute module,” then explained that most of the issues discussed had been published and fixed, but some had not. He declined to provide identifying details for ethical reasons.

Defensive implications

Peter argued that LLMs can rapidly narrow a large search space and repeatedly recombine promising paths until they find a way through. In his view, proprietary formats, hidden sections, and similar barriers no longer offer dependable protection once a capable model has access to the firmware.

He presented two broad responses, rather than a settled remediation plan: - Restrict access to firmware and hope to limit analysis. He cited a post he attributed to Matthew Green recommending the immediate removal of publicly available firmware updates, while acknowledging that this was an uncomfortable recommendation for a security researcher. - Favor open, verifiable designs and use intensive analysis before deployment. He pointed to PQ1 as an example and advocated having LLMs scrutinize initial firmware before it reaches devices in the field.

Questions and qualifications

LZ4 and error strings: Peter clarified that LZ4 is a compression algorithm/library. Error strings are separate diagnostic messages that often remain embedded in compiled firmware and provide useful starting points for identifying components.

Privacy and cypherpunk: Peter rejected the idea that privacy was over. He saw the current situation as an opportunity to rebuild insecure systems closer to cypherpunk ideals, although internet-connected devices that cannot be updated might need to be discarded.

Reducing smart-device complexity: He supported eliminating unnecessary features, questioning why a washing machine needs Wi-Fi. He distinguished this from avoiding third-party libraries altogether: although dependencies currently introduce vulnerabilities, he expected secure, battle-tested libraries eventually to become preferable again to in-house replacements.

Perfectly safe hardware: Speaking explicitly as a matter of personal opinion, Peter said security is a spectrum, not an absolute guarantee. Attackers weigh effort and cost against a target’s value; a hardware wallet holding $1 million justifies more investment than a refrigerator. He also argued that future mathematical or physical discoveries could invalidate assumptions considered safe today.

Why Kimi: Peter said willingness to help was the deciding factor, not superior capability. He considered an OpenAI model he called “Astra” better at some security tasks but less willing to assist. His described practice was to use Kimi for the broader investigation and occasionally ask the other model for help with a narrower subtask.

Purpose and central argument

Peter’s talk examined how an LLM can analyze proprietary device firmware, identify vulnerabilities, and combine them into an exploit that bypasses secure boot. He argued that the difficulty humans face when interpreting large amounts of unfamiliar machine code is becoming a much weaker barrier: LLMs can rapidly unpack firmware, identify components, narrow the attack surface, and explore combinations of bugs.

He reported testing Kimi against an undisclosed hardware platform. According to Peter, approximately one hour of analysis using a $100 Kimi subscription found 15 zero-day vulnerabilities and three secure boot escapes. He qualified that the 15 findings were not all necessarily exploitable: some might only cause crashes. Reproducing the findings on actual hardware took him an entire day, making him, rather than the model, the bottleneck.

The hardware manufacturer and exact device were not disclosed. During questions, Peter said most of the issues discussed had already been published and fixed, but some had not, so he would not provide further identifying details.

Firmware as the security boundary

Peter used a generic compute module to represent devices ranging from refrigerators to hardware wallets. He described secure boot as a mechanism that permits a computer to boot only digitally signed operating systems.

He divided the system into: - Internal components, such as the CPU and RAM, which he treated as comparatively protected unless an attacker undertakes physical intervention. - External inputs and storage, including SD cards, NVMe drives, USB devices, and network boot sources, which are less trustworthy. - Firmware, which initializes hardware, configures components, provides drivers, and implements the secure boot checks separating untrusted inputs from the trusted system.

This makes firmware a particularly important target: an error in code that processes external data before signature verification can undermine the protection secure boot is intended to provide.

Extracting firmware physically can require desoldering chips, using readers, and other difficult, error-prone work. Peter explained that this is often unnecessary because manufacturers distribute firmware updates online, sometimes through GitHub. An attacker or researcher may therefore obtain the relevant firmware without touching a device.

He said some mainstream models refuse to assist with this kind of investigation, whereas Kimi was willing to analyze his hardware and firmware.

Unpacking and understanding proprietary firmware

Peter described an analysis sequence in which a model first disassembles a firmware image. Initially, substantial portions may remain uninterpretable because they are compressed or transformed rather than directly executable code.

The model can examine the readable portions to identify the routines that unpack the rest. In his example: - It recognized an LZ4 decompression algorithm and used it to expose additional sections. Peter said this step took approximately five seconds. - It then identified a proprietary data-transformation algorithm and applied that to the remaining hidden section. - The proprietary firmware was fully exposed approximately five minutes after analysis began.

His argument was that a device must itself contain some means of interpreting its firmware, and an LLM can use those mechanisms to reverse the concealment. Proprietary formats and unfamiliar transformations therefore did not provide a durable obstacle in the example.

Once the code was available, embedded error messages supplied useful semantic anchors. Messages associated with HTTP, DNS, and other libraries helped the model identify what otherwise unfamiliar code sections did. From those anchors, it could construct a call graph and map information flow through the firmware.

The next important boundary was the secure boot check itself. Peter focused on code reachable before that check, where externally controlled input could influence execution before an operating system had been authenticated. The model mapped inputs such as SD-card contents and NVMe data to the firmware components that process them.

Reducing the vulnerability search space

Peter emphasized that simply asking a model to find bugs throughout a large firmware image leaves too much complexity. His described approach progressively reduced the problem.

First, the model searched for a limited set of suspicious patterns, without assuming they were exploitable. Examples included possible buffer overflows, problematic loops, incomplete conditional handling, logical errors, and unexpected interactions between identified components. Recognizing libraries from their error strings also allowed comparison with known vulnerabilities in vulnerability databases.

This produced a smaller collection of candidate sites, but important questions remained: Could an attacker reach them? Could the attacker control the relevant data? Would the result be useful exploitation or merely a crash?

The next stages addressed those questions: - Examine the consequences of reaching each suspicious site. Peter said many candidates would simply crash the processor; in his illustrative account, this eliminated roughly half. - Trace backward from each candidate through its callers rather than trying to explore every possible forward path from an input. - Eliminate branches blocked by validation checks or branches that are unreachable or dead code. - Retain paths that lead back to attacker-controlled input.

Peter described this vulnerability-tracing stage as static analysis: the model inspected code and modeled branches and jumps without running the actual firmware. It could create a small Python representation of the control flow.

Static reasoning was not sufficient for every candidate. Arithmetic, data movement, and transformations could make an apparently viable path fail or behave differently than expected. The model therefore simulated relevant firmware behavior in Python and stepped through the paths to distinguish usable vulnerabilities from false leads. Peter used five remaining vulnerabilities as an illustrative intermediate result, not as the final count from his reported test.

Turning reachable bugs into an exploit chain

A vulnerability in a component does not automatically mean a deployed device will expose that component to an attacker. Peter gave the example of a bootloader configured to use only an SD card: a flaw in its NVMe driver would not necessarily be reachable. Likewise, identifying a DNS-related vulnerability leaves the question of how to induce the bootloader to perform a DNS lookup.

He described the model’s next task as recombining vulnerabilities and execution paths until it found a chain connecting an accessible input to a useful failure.

The example chain involved network boot: - The compute module could retrieve its operating system over HTTP. - An attacker-controlled HTTP response could trigger an error and move the firmware into a separate retry implementation. - A subsequent redirect could send execution down another pathway and eventually cause a DNS lookup. - Peter attributed these additional pathways to functionality inherited from an included library that had not been removed from the firmware. - The DNS-handling code copied a response into a 64-byte buffer. An oversized response of approximately 5 kilobytes could overwrite firmware memory.

Peter presented the resulting outcome as remote code execution and a full secure boot bypass. The larger point was that seemingly secondary error-handling and fallback behavior could make an otherwise unexpected vulnerable component reachable before the secure boot check.

Reported results and their limits

Peter said he had expected Kimi to find nothing when he initially ran it against the hardware. Instead, he reported: - 15 zero-day vulnerabilities, with varying or unresolved exploitability. - Three secure boot escapes, which he characterized as catastrophic for a hardware project. - Approximately one hour for the automated scan on a $100 Kimi subscription. - An entire day of his own work to reproduce the findings on hardware.

The talk did not provide the device identity or a finding-by-finding account of what had been reproduced. Peter’s distinction between potential vulnerabilities, crash-only bugs, and secure boot escapes remained important to interpreting the headline numbers.

His broader conclusion was that LLMs can cheaply narrow a large problem to a small set of promising sites, then persistently explore and recombine those sites until they find a successful path.

Defensive implications

Peter recalled a post he attributed, with some uncertainty, to Matthew Green suggesting that publicly available firmware updates should immediately be removed from the internet. He called that both an uncomfortable recommendation for a security researcher and potentially the best immediate defensive move available.

He argued that once an LLM has the firmware, proprietary packaging, concealment, or encryption should not be assumed to stop it from eventually exposing the code. He framed two broad options: - Hide the firmware, restrict access, and hope that this delays attacks. - Pursue open, verifiable systems and use intensive analysis, including LLM analysis, to harden firmware before widespread deployment. He cited PQ1 as an example of the direction he favored.

He did not present either option as a guarantee. Concealment was framed as an immediate expedient, while openness depended on finding and addressing weaknesses early.

Questions and clarifications

LZ4 and error messages

Asked what “LZ4 error” meant, Peter clarified that LZ4 is a compression algorithm or library. The relevant error messages are ordinary developer-facing strings embedded in libraries, not a special kind of LZ4 vulnerability. Such strings often remain in compiled firmware and become valuable starting points for identifying components and understanding the image.

Which devices he had hacked

Peter jokingly described the target as a generic blue compute module that did not exist. He then made clear that he should not identify it. Most issues had been published and fixed, but some remained undisclosed or unresolved, making further detail inappropriate in his view.

Whether privacy and cypherpunk were over

Peter rejected that conclusion. He described the current period as a potential renaissance for privacy and cypherpunk: existing systems had proved weaker than hoped, but there was an opportunity to rebuild from the ground up.

He acknowledged that some internet-connected household devices that cannot be updated might need to be discarded. Nevertheless, he expected rebuilt systems to align more closely with cypherpunk goals than current ones.

Whether smart devices should be simplified

Peter distinguished two interpretations: - Removing unnecessary features: he supported this, questioning why a washing machine needs Wi-Fi. - Replacing external dependencies with in-house implementations: he did not endorse this as a permanent answer. He viewed the current prevalence of vulnerable libraries as potentially temporary and suggested that, once hardened, battle-tested libraries could again be safer than custom replacements.

Whether any hardware can be perfectly safe

Peter explicitly framed his answer as personal opinion. He said security is a spectrum and that the practical question is whether the effort and expense required to break a system are justified by the reward.

A hardware wallet holding $1 million could justify substantial attack expenditure; a refrigerator would ordinarily be less valuable, though someone might attack it for amusement. He did not believe perfect, permanent safety could be established, because future mathematical or physical discoveries might introduce attack methods not accounted for today. He offered no definitive test or certification that would prove a device could withstand LLM-assisted exploitation.

Why Kimi

Peter’s main reason was willingness to assist rather than superior technical capability. He said Kimi accepted that the hardware belonged to him and was therefore willing to help investigate it.

He also said that an OpenAI model he called “Astra” was better at some of this work but more reluctant to help. His described workflow was to use Kimi for the broader investigation and occasionally ask Astra to handle a narrowly scoped part. Thus, his choice reflected access and refusal behavior as well as technical performance.
